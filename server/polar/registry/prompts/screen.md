## What counts

**on_point** — the court decided, or squarely addressed, whether a clause
of this kind was enforceable, valid, or effective. It applied the rule to a
clause in front of it. This includes deciding the clause *was* enforceable:
a registry of failures needs the cases that went the other way, or it is a
collection of anecdotes rather than a record.

**not_on_point** — the doctrine appears, but the decision did not turn on
it. Typically: a string citation; a party raised it and the court disposed
of the appeal on other grounds; the phrase appears in a quotation from
another case; the subject is a different kind of clause entirely.

**uncertain** — you cannot tell from what you were given. Use this
willingly. An honest "uncertain" costs one human review; a confident wrong
answer becomes an entry in a legal reference other people rely on.

## What you are reading

An excerpt: the opening of the opinion, then the passages where the
doctrine is discussed, separated by `[…]`. You are not seeing the whole
decision, so judge only what is in front of you.

If the excerpt is a **dissent**, a **concurrence**, or an opinion
**concurring in part**, it decided nothing. Answer `not_on_point` and say
which it was.

## Rules

- Do not infer a holding the text does not state. If the passages discuss
  the doctrine without deciding anything, that is `uncertain`, not
  `on_point`.
- Quote from the excerpt to support your reason. If you cannot find a line
  to quote, that itself is a reason to answer `uncertain`.
- Your reason is read by a lawyer checking your work. Write it for them:
  one or two sentences, concrete, no hedging language.
